Chronic pain is real. The sensations you feel are not imagined. Pain that lasts for months or years can change how you move, sleep, work, and think about your body.

## What the brain does with pain

Pain can work like an early alarm system, warning you that something could be wrong. In the short term, it protects you. When you get hurt, the brain raises the alarm so you take care of yourself while you heal.

In some people, the brain can learn to keep sending that alarm after the body has healed, or even when the body is no longer in any danger. When this occurs in the brain it's called neuroplasticity, or the brain's ability to change itself. The good news is that the same ability that lets the brain learn pain signals also lets it learn that those signals are actually safe.

Having neuroplastic conditions does not mean the pain is "all in your head." The pain is definitely real. For some people, the brain can keep generating pain even after an injury has healed or even after numerous medical tests do not show a root cause. In some cases, pain can have an ongoing physical cause along with neuroplastic conditions and many people have a mix of both when dealing with chronic pain. It's important to understand where you are to begin your healing journey and where the brain is creating false pain signals.

This is why learning the brain's role is the first idea in [the process](https://excelsior-prt.com/#process) we use in coaching.

## How fear feeds the cycle

When pain lasts, it is natural to become afraid of it. You may worry that a movement will make it worse. You are likely paying closer attention to your body for the next flare-up. This preoccupation with the pain can lead to avoiding things you used to enjoy, significantly impacting your life.

Each of these reactions makes sense. At the same time, they can also tell the brain that the body is under threat. A brain that senses constant danger can keep the alarm switched on, and a louder alarm can mean more pain, which can bring more fear. This is often called the pain-fear cycle. It is not your fault. It is a learned pattern that many people fall into, and learned patterns can change.

Stress, worry, and strong emotions can feed the same loop. Many people notice that their pain shifts with stress, mood, rest, or situation. Again, the pain is still real, but your brain is sending false signals based on different life conditions. When this occurs, it is a clear sign that the nervous system is playing a part in the pain-fear cycle as nervous system regulation is a key part of healing from chronic pain caused by neuroplasticity.

### Patterns some people notice

You might recognize some of these:

- Your pain changes with your stress level or mood, or situation.
- You feel tense or on guard when you expect pain.
- You avoid movement or activities because you fear a flare.
- You find yourself watching your body closely for sensations.

## What Pain Reprocessing Therapy teaches

Pain Reprocessing Therapy (PRT) is an approach that teaches you to meet the signals from your body with less fear. The aim is to interrupt the pain-fear cycle and dial down your nervous system's sensitivity.

Some of the practices include:

- **Learning the brain's role.** Understanding how pain works may help lower fear.
- **Finding your own evidence.** You notice when your pain shifts and what moves it.
- **Somatic tracking.** You pay attention to a sensation with calm curiosity instead of fear, while reminding yourself that it is safe.
- **Facing what is underneath.** You work with the stress, worry, and emotions that can keep the alarm switched on.
- **Moving toward what feels good.** You notice and build positive feelings, so the brain gets more safe signals.

For some people, as the brain relearns safety, the alarm may quiet down. Results vary, and no one can promise one. What this approach offers is a way to practice meeting pain differently.
